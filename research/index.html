How do animals choose the their mates? What signals do they use?
When are males and females expected to cooperate, and when should they be at each others throats?
How does each sex protect their own interests?
And why bother with all this in the first place?

I am interested in how ecological and evolutionary processes shape the bizarre world of sex and sexual interactions. I use mainly the tools of mathematical modelling and computer simulations to get to the underlying theory that can account for the patterns we observe.

In the past few years I focused on the problem of sexual conflict and post-mating sexual selection. Primarily, the conflict over the action of seminal fluid proteins. Those proteins are provided by males to females during mating in Drosophila and many (most?) animal species. Since 1995 at least, they were a poster example of traits that males use to manipulate females. Yet more recently, people argue that the reality might not be so simple. The work we did (with David Duneau and Jean-Baptiste Ferdy), and the models we created suggest that those proteins can act as cues or signals and could be rather beneficial to females, providing us an amazing example of post-mating sexual signal.
